Original statement

“Compare it to Taiwan, a country with about the same population – and a similar size economy – that faces a vastly larger security threat. We are spending as much as twice what Taiwan spends on military expenditure.”

28 April 2024 · Video posted on X

Original source

  • Tier 1 · PrimaryTranscript· 28 April 2024
    Video posted on X, 28 April 2024twitter.com

    David Shoebridge (X)

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

Stockholm International Peace Research Institute data for 2023 record Australian military spending of US$32.3 billion against Taiwan's US$16.6 billion, roughly double. Australia's nominal GDP (about US$1.87 trillion) is, however, more than twice Taiwan's (about US$802 billion), so Taiwan spends more as a share of its economy (2.2 per cent against 1.9 per cent). The 'similar size economy' comparison holds only on a purchasing-power-parity basis (CIA World Factbook: Taiwan US$1.14 trillion in 2019, Australia US$1.33 trillion in 2022), which three experts told AAP FactCheck was a problematic basis for comparing military spending because hardware is bought in US dollars and Taiwan relies on conscription. On a consistent purchasing-power basis the Lowy Institute put 2022 spending at US$36.9 billion for Australia and US$30 billion for Taiwan.

Context

AAP FactCheck rated other figures in the same video accurate, including that Australia's per-capita military spending had roughly doubled since 2003 and was about three times Japan's and six times China's. Original statement

“selling weapons, after weapons, after weapons to Israel”

7 November 2023 · Senate, debate to take note of answers to questions without notice

Original source

  • Tier 1 · PrimaryHansard· 7 November 2023
    Senate Hansard, 7 November 2023: questions without notice, take note of answers (Trade: Department of Defence)parlinfo.aph.gov.au

    Parliament of Australia

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

Defence officials told Senate estimates on 25 October 2023 that 351 export permits relating to Israel had been issued since 1 January 2017, but that permits cover 'military and dual-use' goods such as radios, sporting equipment and vehicle parts, and that none of the 322 permits issued between January 2017 and 31 March 2023 was for 'lethal equipment'. ABS trade data showed exports to Israel in the 'arms and ammunition' category averaging about $2.6 million a year over the five years to 2022 and $8,000 in the first nine months of 2023; Australia's Arms Trade Treaty reports record six 'small arms and light weapons' sent in 2016 and 2017. Defence Minister Richard Marles said on 7 November 2023 that 'Israel has not sought any weapons from Australia and we have not provided any'. Defence analysts told Fact Check the export data are opaque, and an editor's note added on 13 December 2023 said the available data may not capture inputs to international supply chains, such as components for weapons systems.

Context

RMIT ABC Fact Check published this analysis in its CheckMate newsletter without a verdict label. PUBLIC RECORD classifies the claim as mixed: the available official data do not show sales of complete weapons to Israel in the period, while the permit system covers a broad range of military and dual-use goods and the data do not capture components supplied through international supply chains. No direct response from Senator Shoebridge to the fact-check is recorded; the Greens subsequently tabled a petition calling for an end to military exports to Israel.
