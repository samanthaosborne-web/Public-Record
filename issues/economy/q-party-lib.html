18 August 2026 · House of Representatives, Question Time (question to the Prime Minister)

Original source

  • Tier 1 · PrimaryHansard· 18 August 2026
    House of Representatives Hansard, 18 August 2026: questions without noticeaph.gov.au

    Parliament of Australia

Evidence status

Contradicted by evidenceThe best available evidence is inconsistent with the claim as stated.

NAB's 17 August 2026 economic newsletter described an illustrative scenario: if rental yields rose by one percentage point and property prices stayed flat, Sydney and Melbourne rents would need to rise by 25 to 30 per cent. It said any adjustment was more likely to combine lower dwelling prices and higher rents. In a note issued on 18 August, before the question was asked, NAB stated that the figure 'was not a forecast'. Ray White, whose modelling produced a similar figure, said its scenario assumed no price falls, which it considered unlikely, and was 'not a forecast of rent growth'. No published NAB material forecast a 30 per cent rise in rents.

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

Senator Hume's office said the figure compares total payments forecast in the 2022 Pre-election Economic and Fiscal Outlook with those in the 2024-25 Mid-Year Economic and Fiscal Outlook for 2023-24 to 2027-28. “In the first 18 months of Labor's time in office, personal income tax had increased by a record 27 per cent”

15 January 2024 · Opinion piece in The Australian

Original source

  • Tier 2 · IndependentNews report· 15 January 2024
    Opinion piece: 'Australians deserve unambiguous commitment to stage three tax cuts'theaustralian.com.au

    The Australian (opinion piece by Angus Taylor)

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

ABS national accounts show income tax paid by individuals rose 27.3 per cent in nominal terms between the March quarter 2022 ($71.5 billion) and the September quarter 2023 ($91.0 billion). That period includes about two months of the previous Coalition government; from the June quarter 2022 the rise was 23.9 per cent. The rise was not a record: larger 18-month increases occurred in 1963-65 (43.5 per cent), 1969-70 (40.9 per cent), 1973-74 (107.2 per cent) and in the first 18 months of the Fraser government (41.7 per cent). Measured as a share of gross household income, income tax rose from 15.1 to 17.2 per cent, an increase of 13.6 per cent, which was also not a record.
