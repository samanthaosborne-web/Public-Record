Funding data supplied to AAP FactCheck by Foodbank Australia showed its total federal funding fell from $1.4 million in 2013-14 to $1.3 million in 2014-15, $1.05 million in 2015-16 and $750,000 a year from 2016-17 to 2018-19, before rising to $16.2 million in 2019-20 during the pandemic. Parliamentary records show the Coalition voted against the December 2022 legislation that created the Energy Bill Relief Fund and funded the first round, although the then Opposition Leader said it would have supported the subsidies if they had been separated from the coal and gas price caps in the same bill. The third round, announced in the 2025-26 budget, lapsed at the dissolution of Parliament, was reintroduced in July 2025, and the then Shadow Treasurer had said in March 2025 the Coalition would not oppose it. AAP FactCheck found no evidence the Coalition opposed the second or third rounds.

“The last time government changed in Australia, in 2013, in the 2014 budget $30 billion was ripped out of public schools in that budget”

April 2025 · Televised leaders' debates during the 2025 election campaign (Sky News and ABC debates; exact dates not stated in the fact-check)

Original source

  • Tier 2 · IndependentTranscript
    Sky News leaders' debate, April 2025 (video, from 1:23)youtu.be

    Sky News Australia (YouTube)

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

The figure traces to a 2014-15 Treasury estimate, given in answer to a Senate estimates question, that replacing Labor's school funding indexation with indexation to inflation plus enrolment growth would reduce projected Commonwealth spending by about $28 billion between 2017-18 and 2024-25. That was a reduction in the projected rate of future increases, not a cut to existing funding. The 2014 budget committed to the four funded years of Labor's 2013 plan; Labor's six-year plan had only about a third of its cost budgeted and four jurisdictions signed up. Public finance experts consulted by AAP FactCheck described the change as a re-estimation of unfunded commitments rather than a cut.

  • Tier 1 · PrimaryHansard· 4 February 2025
    House of Representatives Hansard, 4 February 2025parlinfo.aph.gov.au

    Parliament of Australia

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

The figure originated in analysis by the Treasurer's office, which combined ABS average weekly earnings, the 2024 tax cuts and energy bill relief, without adjusting for inflation, and assumed that average earnings growth over the Coalition's 2013-2022 term would simply have continued. Economists consulted by AAP FactCheck said real wages, measured by the wage price index less inflation, fell for most of the government's term and had only recently turned positive; one inflation-adjusted calculation showed real earnings essentially unchanged under both governments. They also said the counterfactual ignored low unemployment and independent Fair Work Commission and Reserve Bank decisions.

“That's 790,000 new jobs created. More than any first term government on record.”

26 March 2024 · Post on X (video)

Original source

  • Tier 1 · PrimaryTranscript
    Post on Xtwitter.com

    Anthony Albanese (X)

Evidence status

Mixed / context requiredThe claim is accurate under some definitions, periods or measures but not others. The context matters.

ABS labour force data show there were 789,700 more employed people in February 2024 than in May 2022, the largest such increase for a first-term government since 1966, the earliest year for which broadly comparable data exist. Labour economists told RMIT ABC Fact Check that raw numbers ignore population growth: adjusted for population, the increase in employment during the first 21 months of the Hawke government in 1983-84 was larger, and that government also fared better on hours worked, underemployment and the share of new jobs that were full-time. Experts also said governments cannot take sole credit for job creation, most of which occurs in the private sector.
